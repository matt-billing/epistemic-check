# The Ten Checks

The operational version of the ten-step framework. Apply the checks the claim triggers; skip the rest.

## 1. Separate the claim from the framing

Most online arguments mix three things without labelling them: the **claim** (what's asserted), the **evidence** (what's offered to support it), and the **frame** (the emotional or political meaning attached).

"Britain is being flooded with migrants": the claim might be about numbers, the evidence might be asylum data, and the frame is the word "flooded". Nobody wants to be flooded. The frame works in every direction: "strengthened by immigration" is a frame too.

Find the plain factual claim underneath the rhetoric. It is usually weaker, narrower, or less certain than the framing suggests. In this example, the accurate stripped version is: migration to Britain is high by some relevant measure. Which measure? That's check 3.

## 2. Know what kind of claim it is

- **Simple factual** ("An earthquake happened", "The PM said this") — often checkable. Go and check.
- **Statistical** ("Crime is rising", "Migration is a net fiscal benefit") — needs definitions, data sources, timeframes, comparison points before it can even be evaluated.
- **Causal** ("Phones are making children anxious") — much harder. Correlation is not causation. Needs argument, not just data.
- **Interpretive** ("This proves institutional failure") — may be reasonable, may even be true, but it's an argument, not a fact.
- **Moral** ("This is unjust") — can't be settled by evidence alone, though evidence can strengthen or weaken it.

Claims usually stack: a statistic wrapped in a causal claim wrapped in a moral one. Unstack before evaluating, and evaluate each layer by its own standard.

## 3. Demand the missing denominator

"Ten thousand people did X" means almost nothing without: out of how many? Compared to what? Compared to when? Per capita or total? Absolute or a rate? Nominal or inflation-adjusted? Gross or net? Budget allocated or actual spend?

Crime can rise in total while falling per person. Cash figures can rise while real value falls. GDP can grow while living standards fall. Never accept a number until you know its denominator, timeframe, and definition.

## 4. Watch for the category shift

Dishonest argument quietly changes category midway: "some migrants commit crimes" becomes "migrants are criminal"; "a person questioned asylum policy" becomes "they hate asylum seekers"; "a person criticised Israel" becomes "they support Hamas."

The trick is moving from a specific behaviour to a broad moral identity. Test: has the argument shifted from what someone *did* to what they *are*? If so, flag it.

## 5. Use source discipline

Source matters: a publication with a track record of distortion deserves more scrutiny, as does a statistician funded by the sugar industry. What source cannot do is settle whether the underlying claim is true.

The working principle: a suspicious source means "verify this carefully", never "this must be false." The carrier can be compromised while the claim is sound; official inquiries have repeatedly confirmed stories that tainted outlets ran first.

Primary sources (datasets, court documents, transcripts, full speeches, papers) beat screenshots, summaries, headlines, and viral posts, but they still need interpretation: a real dataset can be selectively chosen, an accurate quote stripped of context.

Screenshots are among the weakest evidence online: croppable, editable, misdatable, parody-sourceable. A screenshot triggers one question: can the original be found? If not, lower confidence. Not necessarily false, just lower.

## 6. Track incentives

Why is this person showing you this? Clicks, outrage revenue, tribe defence, institutional protection, fundraising, elections, status, score-settling, or the truth. Incentives don't make someone wrong; they shape the claim. Engagement-paid accounts profit from outrage, so expect overstatement even when the core is real.

Everyone is biased, so "are they biased?" is the wrong question. The right ones: what would this source be reluctant to admit? What does it want me to see? That's where the blind spots live.

## 7. Real is not the same as representative

A genuine video of one person behaving badly may show one person, or a trend, or nothing at all. The internet constantly turns anecdotes into sociology: one event becomes a pattern, one person becomes a type.

The question that neutralises most viral content: even if this happened, how representative is it? Find the base rate if one exists.

## 8. Apply symmetry

People are forensic with claims they dislike and generous with claims they like: they spot the missing denominator only when the conclusion suits the other side, demand primary sources from opponents and accept screenshots from allies.

The test: would this evidence be acceptable coming from the other side? Applying different standards to structurally identical cases is **asymmetry**. Whoever applies principle X in case A is committed to it in case B, unless they can state the relevant difference. Not gesture at it. State it.

## 9. Notice what is missing

The strongest counterargument, the actual source, the base rate, the uncertainty, the trade-off. A strong claim survives contact with inconvenient facts; a weak claim needs those facts removed. Absence of receipts is itself a finding.

## 10. Hold confidence levels, not binary verdicts

Instead of "true or false?", ask "how confident should I be?"

- **Very low**: screenshot, anonymous, no source, emotionally loaded
- **Low**: single source, unclear context, plausible but unverified
- **Moderate**: multiple credible sources, details uncertain
- **High**: primary source, clear definitions, independently confirmed
- **Very high**: directly documented, uncontested, stable record

Mature judgement often sounds like: "Something like this may be true, but this particular version is probably overstated."

---

One habit underwrites all ten: know what would change your mind. End every analysis by stating it.
