# The Harder Cases

Read this when the argument is about the arguer rather than the claim: when someone dismisses by source, claims authority from experience, or uses an agreed moral premise as leverage. The governing principle: an argument's validity depends on its internal logic, the quality of its evidence, and whether its claims are falsifiable. Not on who makes it.

**Use these labels only when their defining condition is met.** Each concept below has a specific trigger: moral judo requires an agreed moral premise being used as leverage AND an identifiable hidden step; the stakes-to-conclusion gap requires someone claiming authority from lived experience; the immunity clause requires source-category dismissal replacing evaluation. A weak argument that fits none of these is just a weak argument — describe the actual problem in plain words rather than reaching for the nearest named concept. Misapplied labels cost credibility: a reader who knows what moral judo means will distrust the whole analysis when the label is wrong.

## The disconfirmation test

The strongest test of a belief is not whether evidence for it can be found (it almost always can) but: what evidence would weaken it?

If no possible evidence could weaken the claim, it is functioning as identity or faith rather than analysis. If every inconvenient fact is dismissed as fabricated, biased, or beside the point, the position is unfalsifiable. Healthy reasoning sounds like: "I still think this is broadly true, but that fact weakens my version of it."

When evaluating someone's position, ask what would change their mind. When delivering your own verdict, state what would change yours.

## The immunity clause

The disconfirmation test's specific failure mode. When source-evaluation replaces argument-evaluation entirely, it becomes an immunity clause: the claim is pre-processed as tainted and never examined. This is not a conclusion arrived at by reasoning; it is a gate that prevents reasoning from starting.

"That's a right-wing talking point" (or left-wing, or mainstream-media, or conspiracy) is a categorisation, not an argument. The categorisation may even be accurate. It is never a substitute for evaluating what's actually being said.

The tell: when someone cannot state what evidence would change their mind, not because the claim is trivially settled but because every route to it runs through a carrier they've pre-classified as tainted, their framework is closed. This differs from ordinary bias. The defect is **foreclosure**: arriving at the conclusion has been made systematically impossible because the paths to it were blocked in advance.

When you spot an immunity clause in material you're evaluating, name it and then do the work it was blocking: evaluate the claim itself.

## The stakes-to-conclusion gap

Direct experience is strong evidence. Someone who lived through a thing has information no secondary source fully replicates, and that deserves more weight, not less.

What experience does not do is licence the conclusion automatically. "This was mismanaged and we bore the costs" is close to what lived experience directly supports. The leap to a structural claim about what caused it, or what policy should follow, is a different kind of argument needing more than the experience itself. The gap between what experience constitutes evidence *for* and the conclusion drawn *from* it is where the honest argument lives. Locate that gap explicitly.

It cuts both ways: dismissing an argument *because* the speaker has personal stakes is the same error as accepting it because of them. Proximity makes an argument more worth examining. It is not a verdict.

## Moral judo

A rhetorical technique to detect and name.

**The setup:** a moral premise with near-universal agreement. "Collective punishment is wrong." "Children deserve to be safe." Nobody argues with that.

**The throw:** the agreed premise is used as leverage to carry the listener to a contested policy conclusion, fast, often in one sentence: "Collective punishment is wrong, so why are we blaming an entire community for one man's actions?"

The weight used is the listener's own moral seriousness: the more they care about the premise, the harder the throw lands, and resisting looks like rejecting the moral premise itself.

**The hidden step** is where the work happens. In the example: the equation of policy scrutiny with collective punishment. That equation is assumed, never argued. The move works precisely because it stays implicit; stated plainly ("asking why this person was granted entry = blaming everyone of his background"), it becomes evaluable, and usually does not hold.

**The defence:** agree loudly with the moral premise, then refuse to be carried. "Yes, collective punishment is wrong. Now show how this question constitutes collective punishment." Name the hidden step and require it to be argued.

Do not take the wrong immunity: rejecting the moral premise outright concedes the framing and switches off the part of the listener that moral argument should reach. The goal is to stay in the argument while refusing the throw.

When you detect moral judo in material under evaluation, make the hidden step explicit in the output and assess whether it survives being stated.
